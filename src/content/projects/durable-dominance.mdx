How concentrated is championship success, and how much of that concentration is
explained by the teams simply being better? The NBA carries the weight — it is the only
league here with a verified champion for every season from 1980–81 to 2022–23 and a
complete head-to-head matrix — with NCAA football, golf, cycling, tennis, cricket and
the Women's World Cup extending the comparison.

**Fourteen franchises won the last 43 NBA titles. A random walk driven by each season's
real team strengths says there should have been twenty.**

## The hypothesis did not survive

The study began from a counterintuitive premise: that a *more* balanced league might
reinforce the dominance of a few elite teams rather than erode it. Testing it properly
means six backward-looking measures of how decorated each champion was against eight
measures of how tight its league was — 84 correlations from one dataset, so they are
read against a Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery correction rather than raw p-values,
and both sides are detrended on year first, because pedigree counts accumulate
mechanically and league balance drifts with expansion.

| | count |
| --- | --- |
| pairs tested | 84 |
| significant raw | 8 |
| significant detrended | 7 (≈4 expected by chance) |
| **surviving FDR (q ≤ 0.10)** | **3** |

All three survivors are NCAA football, and every one points the same way: **less**
balanced seasons crown more decorated champions. Imbalance and entrenchment travel
together — the intuitive direction, and the opposite of the premise. Nothing survives in
the NBA at all.

That is the honest end of the original question, and the reason the project moved to a
different one.

## A test with a null model behind it

A postseason is a short random walk. Each series is seven Bernoulli draws weighted by
the strength gap plus home court; win four and advance. Feed each season's *real*
strengths through it and you get the title distribution a league should produce if
nothing carried across seasons except quality already visible in the standings.

Strength is a penalised Bradley-Terry MLE on the full head-to-head matrix, so a team is
credited for *who* it beat, not just how often it won. The matrix is complete and
reconciles exactly with the standings — 82 games per team, zero mismatches.

**The model is calibrated.** Scaling every strength gap by a temperature γ and
maximising the likelihood of the 43 real champions returns γ = 1.0: the raw fitted gaps
need no adjustment. The likelihood is flat from 0.8 to 1.4, so that pins the scale only
loosely; the reliability curve is the sharper check, and it tracks the diagonal across
all six probability bins — teams given a 52% chance won 53% of the time.

**And reality is still far more concentrated.** Drawing 20,000 alternate 43-season
leagues:

| Statistic | Real | Simulated median | 95% interval | p |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| unique champions | **14** | 20 | 16 – 23 | 0.004 |
| title HHI | **0.120** | 0.073 | 0.058 – 0.098 | 0.001 |
| top team's share | **0.233** | 0.140 | 0.093 – 0.209 | 0.004 |
| title entropy | **2.345** | 2.781 | 2.551 – 2.976 | 0.0004 |
| back-to-back rate | **0.286** | 0.143 | 0.048 – 0.262 | 0.015 |

All five outside the interval, all in the same direction, and the result holds for all
three strength estimators (Bradley-Terry, margin of victory, raw record) with or without
home court — p ≤ 0.004 in every combination.

## Calibration is marginal; concentration is joint

The obvious objection is that the simulation already uses real strengths, and its
per-season predictions are calibrated, so how can the 43-season record disagree with it?

Both can be true. Calibration is a property of each season taken on its own;
concentration is a property of the sequence. A model can be perfectly calibrated per
team-season and still under-produce dynasties, and the gap means championship outcomes
are correlated across seasons for the same franchise *beyond* what measured strength
explains. That gap is the object of study rather than an error in it.

What the residual is not:

- **Not persistent quality.** Lag-1 autocorrelation of strength is ρ = 0.58, and it is already priced in — the simulation reads each season's real strengths. Within-franchise variation (SD 0.61) dwarfs between-franchise variation (SD 0.23): franchises cycle rather than being permanently good.
- **Not a stable franchise trait.** Splitting history in half and correlating each franchise's excess titles across the two halves gives r = +0.24, p = 0.23, n = 28. Positive, not significant.
- **Not the modern league.** The effect is strongest in 1995–2009 (p = 0.003) and **absent in 2010–2023**, where the real and simulated counts are identical.

Sweeping a fixed per-franchise strength bonus, the observed concentration is reproduced
at a bonus of about 0.38 log-odds — roughly 57% of the within-season spread of team
strength itself. A latent factor that large is itself a reason to doubt the
single-latent-trait story.

## Machine learning does not beat the physics

Predicting the champion from regular-season features, trained on 1980–2008 and tested on
2009–2023:

| Model | Held-out log-loss | AUC |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Random-walk simulation** | **0.1713** | 0.890 |
| Random forest | 0.1717 | 0.893 |
| Logistic regression | 0.1767 | 0.869 |
| Gradient boosting | 0.1990 | 0.865 |
| Uniform over the playoff field | 0.2383 | 0.500 |

The generative simulation, with no learned parameters, matches or beats every learned
model, and adding it as a feature to a logistic regression does not help — it already
contains what the other features carry. Permutation importance puts conference seed
first; prior titles contribute ~0 and career win percentage is *negative*. Franchise
history adds nothing once current quality is known, and a history-only model reaches AUC
0.540 out of sample. **You cannot predict which franchises will beat the random walk
from their history.**

## A cross-sport atlas

Field sizes range from 6 to 143, so each sport is scored against its own null: draw a
champion uniformly from the field that actually competed that year, 20,000 times,
preserving the real sequence of field sizes. The dominance multiple is real
concentration ÷ null concentration.

| Sport | Editions | Median field | Unique champions | Dominance multiple |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tour de France | 44 | 143 | 23 | **3.14×** |
| NCAA football | 54 | 120 | 20 | 2.89× |
| NBA | 43 | 29 | 14 | 2.23× |
| Women's World Cup | 9 | 16 | 5 | 2.09× |
| Tennis majors | 292 | 6 | 83 | 1.79× |
| Masters golf | 54 | 50 | 37 | 1.71× |
| Cricket World Cup | 12 | 11 | 5 | 1.70× |

Every sport is significantly more concentrated than chance among its own entrants.
Individual endurance sport is the most dominated; short-format team tournaments the
least.

## Auditing the first version of this work

The analysis was rebuilt from the raw data rather than extended, because re-running the
original scripts turned up three defects worth publishing:

- **Row-order misalignment.** The NFL script built dominance from `df['Year'].unique()` — file order, descending — and balance from `groupby('Year')`, ascending, then sliced both positionally. Every season's balance was paired with a different season's dominance. Fixing it flips the slope from +23.3 (p = 0.022) to −9.8 (p = 0.33).
- **A spurious trend.** NCAA football dominance was defined as a cumulative count, so it is 85% explained by year alone and correlates with anything else that trends. R² against rating spread falls from 0.49 to 0.014 (p = 0.39) once both series are detrended.
- **In-sample reporting.** Every R² in the original deck was in-sample. Under forward-chaining cross-validation, no model — linear or ensemble — reaches positive out-of-sample R² at the season level.

The original exploratory scripts are left untouched in the repository as the historical
record, with an appendix explaining which of their measures were safe to reuse and why.

## What would settle it

Actual playoff series results, so the model can be tested where it matters rather than
calibrated to one outcome per year; roster continuity, the leading candidate mechanism
and one the standings cannot see; and more leagues with verified champions, since the
concentration test needs long records to have power.
