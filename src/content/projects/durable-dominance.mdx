An empirical study of whether competitive balance and durable dominance actually trade
off, across more than fifteen leagues and competitions — and a Monte Carlo season
simulator built to test the same question on synthetic leagues where the true skill
spread is known by construction.

The hypothesis under test is counterintuitive: **that as competition becomes more
balanced, the dominance of a few top teams may be reinforced rather than eroded.**

## The two phases

**Measurement.** Collect season-level data — match and race results, end-of-season
rankings, market values for soccer, rosters for MLB — and plot competitive balance
against a dominance index for each league. Balance is measured several ways (upset
percentage, score difference, season-points HHI, relative standard deviation of points,
standard deviation of win percentage, margin-of-victory spread) because no single proxy
survives contact with every sport.

**Simulation.** Model each team's skill as a draw from a normal distribution with spread
`s`, resolve matches probabilistically, simulate the season, then fit `s` per season
against the real standings. If the fitted spread tracks real competitive balance, the
model is a usable stand-in for leagues that cannot be re-run.

## Balance against dominance, by measure

Across the five European soccer leagues, the sign depends entirely on which balance
measure you pick — which is the first result worth having.

| Measure | Direction | R² | p |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Season-points RSD | positive | 0.450 | 7.07e-12 |
| Season-points HHI | positive | 0.341 | 7.37e-12 |
| Upset percentage | negative | — | — |
| Score difference | negative | — | — |

Concentration measures (HHI, RSD) rise with dominance; game-level closeness measures
(upsets, score difference) fall with it. A league can look balanced game to game and
still concentrate titles.

## Where the sign flips

MLB runs the other way from soccer. Excluding the first twenty years of the sample:

| Relationship | R² | p |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Upsets percentage vs dominance | 0.263 | 0.0024 |
| Closeness of winning percentage vs dominance | 0.323 | 0.00016 |

In baseball, seasons with *more* upsets and *closer* winning percentages are the seasons
with *more* concentrated historical success — the hypothesis holding in the one sport
with the longest continuous record. In soccer the same comparison points the other way.
That disagreement is the finding: "competitive balance" is not one quantity, and which
way it moves against dominance depends on the sport and the measure together.

## The simulator

Team skill is drawn from a normal distribution; a match resolves as

```
P(Team 1 wins) = c^s1 / (c^s1 + c^s2)
```

with a tie-capable variant for hockey and soccer that adds a draw parameter `b`:

```
P(Team 1 wins) = b·c^s1 / (b·c^s1 + c^s2)
P(Tie)         = 1 − P(Team 1 wins) − P(Team 2 wins)
```

Fitting `c` and the per-season skill spread `s` against real NBA seasons from 1981–1982
to 2022–2023 gives mean squared errors between roughly 2.5 and 12 on team win totals,
with `c` stable at 0.908 across the whole period.

The validation that matters is whether the fitted spread predicts real balance:

| League | Simulated spread vs real spread | R² | p |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NBA | SD of winning percentage | 0.613 | 5.5e-10 |
| NHL | SD of point percentage | 0.403 | 6.3e-06 |

A single fitted parameter explaining 61% of the variance in four decades of real NBA
standings spread is the strongest evidence here that the simulator is modelling the
league rather than fitting noise.

## Data collected

Formula One, college football, the NFL, the NHL, NCAA Men's March Madness, MLB, and the
five major European soccer leagues (Premier League, Bundesliga, Serie A, Ligue 1, La
Liga), plus the Masters, tennis Grand Slams, the Tour de France, cricket, rugby, and both
World Cups as smaller cross-checks.
